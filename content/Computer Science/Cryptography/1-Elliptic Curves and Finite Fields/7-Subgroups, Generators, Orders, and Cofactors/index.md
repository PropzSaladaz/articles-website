[Elliptic Curves Over Finite Fields](/collections/computer-science/cryptography/elliptic-curves-and-finite-fields/elliptic-curves-over-finite-fields/) constructed the finite group

$$
E(\mathbb{F}_{11}):
\qquad
y^2 \equiv x^3+x+1 \pmod{11}.
$$

That construction produced $13$ affine points and the identity point $\mathcal{O}$. It also used the finite-field addition formulas with

$$
P=(1,5)
$$

to calculate

$$
2P=(3,3)
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
3P=(8,2).
$$

We will now continue those exact scalar multiplications around the point cloud. The resulting cycles give concrete meanings to **curve order**, **point order**, **subgroup**, **generator**, **base point**, and **cofactor**.

The main idea resembles the modular clock from [Finite Fields and Modular Arithmetic](/collections/computer-science/cryptography/elliptic-curves-and-finite-fields/finite-fields-and-modular-arithmetic/). Repeatedly adding a fixed number moves around a finite clock; repeatedly adding a fixed curve point moves around a finite set of curve points. In both cases, the chosen step determines which positions we visit before returning to the start.

## Curve Order: The Size of the Complete Group

The **curve order** is the number of points in the complete curve group, including $\mathcal{O}$. It is written

$$
\#E(\mathbb{F}_p).
$$

For the running curve, Chapter 6 found $13$ affine points. Therefore,

$$
\#E(\mathbb{F}_{11})=13+1=14.
$$

This number describes the whole curve group. It is not the field modulus and it is not automatically the order of every point.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> In the running example, $p=11$ is the number of field elements, while $\#E(\mathbb{F}_{11})=14$ is the number of curve points. These numbers measure different objects.

## Point Order: The Length of One Scalar Cycle

[Scalar multiplication](/collections/computer-science/cryptography/elliptic-curves-and-finite-fields/scalar-multiplication/) repeatedly adds the same point:

$$
kP=\underbrace{P+P+\cdots+P}_{k\text{ copies of }P}.
$$

We also define $0P=\mathcal{O}$. Now imagine the $14$ curve points as stops on a circular route. The point $P$ acts like a fixed step: begin at $\mathcal{O}$, add $P$ to move to the next stop, then add $P$ again, and keep going.

$$
\mathcal{O}
\xrightarrow{+P}P
\xrightarrow{+P}2P
\xrightarrow{+P}3P
\xrightarrow{+P}\cdots
$$

This route is an abstract picture of the group operation. It is not a geometric line drawn through the point cloud.

For $P=(1,5)$, Chapter 6 already gave us the first two moves:

$$
P=(1,5),
\qquad
2P=(3,3),
\qquad
3P=(8,2).
$$

We do not need to memorize every coordinate encountered after that. Three landmarks reveal the structure of the route:

- At the halfway point, $7P=(2,0)$. This point is its own inverse because its $y$-coordinate is zero.
- One step before completing the route, $13P=(1,6)=-P$.
- Adding $P$ once more gives $14P=(-P)+P=\mathcal{O}$.

No earlier positive multiple is $\mathcal{O}$. The route therefore makes one complete lap after fourteen additions of $P$.

The **order of a point $P$**, written $\operatorname{ord}(P)$, is the smallest positive integer $n$ for which

$$
nP=\mathcal{O}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\operatorname{ord}(P)=\min\{n>0:nP=\mathcal{O}\}.
$$

For the running point,

$$
\operatorname{ord}(P)=14.
$$

The two uses of the word **order** now answer different questions:

:::compare[Curve order compared with point order]
- **Curve order — total stops**: How many points exist in the complete curve group? Here, $\#E(\mathbb{F}_{11})=14$.
- **Point order — lap length**: How many times do we add this point before returning to $\mathcal{O}$? Here, $\operatorname{ord}(P)=14$.
:::

## Generated Subgroups and Generators

The stops reached before a scalar cycle returns to $\mathcal{O}$ form the **subgroup generated by $P$**:

$$
\langle P\rangle
=
\{\mathcal{O},P,2P,\ldots,(n-1)P\},
$$

where $n=\operatorname{ord}(P)$. This subgroup contains exactly $n$ points.

A point is a **generator** of a group $H$ when its scalar multiples reach every element of $H$:

$$
\langle P\rangle=H.
$$

In the route analogy, $\langle P\rangle$ is the collection of stops reachable by repeatedly taking the $P$-step. A generator of the whole group is a step that eventually visits every stop.

Every point generates its own subgroup, but it does not necessarily generate the complete curve group. For $P=(1,5)$, one lap visits all $14$ curve points. Thus,

$$
\langle P\rangle=E(\mathbb{F}_{11}),
$$

and $P$ is a generator of the whole curve group.

## Select a Smaller Prime-Order Subgroup

Cryptographic systems commonly work inside a carefully selected large prime-order subgroup rather than requiring the base point to generate every point on the curve.

Our toy curve can demonstrate the same structure. Select the point already obtained by doubling $P$ in Chapter 6:

$$
G=2P=(3,3).
$$

Multiples of $G$ are the even multiples of $P$ because

$$
kG=k(2P)=2kP.
$$

On the fourteen-stop route traced by $P$, adding $G=2P$ is like moving two stops at a time:

$$
\mathcal{O}
\xrightarrow{+G}2P
\xrightarrow{+G}4P
\xrightarrow{+G}6P
\xrightarrow{+G}\cdots
\xrightarrow{+G}12P
\xrightarrow{+G}14P=\mathcal{O}.
$$

This new route visits every other stop. After seven $G$-steps, the total movement is

$$
7G=7(2P)=14P=\mathcal{O}.
$$

No earlier $G$-step returns to $\mathcal{O}$, so

$$
\operatorname{ord}(G)=7.
$$

The seven visited stops form $\langle G\rangle$. This is a **proper subgroup**: it lies inside the fourteen-point curve group but does not contain every curve point. The odd multiples $P,3P,5P,\ldots$ belong to the complete group but are skipped by the $G$-route.

## Why Subgroup Orders Divide the Curve Order

The route analogy also suggests why subgroup sizes cannot be arbitrary. A subgroup must fit evenly inside the complete finite group: translated copies of the subgroup partition the complete group into equal-sized sets.

Lagrange's theorem states this precisely. For a point $R$ on an elliptic curve,

$$
\operatorname{ord}(R)\mid\#E(\mathbb{F}_p).
$$

Our three useful step sizes make this visible:

- Adding $P$ visits all fourteen stops before returning, so $\operatorname{ord}(P)=14$.
- Adding $G=2P$ visits every other stop, so $\operatorname{ord}(G)=7$.
- Adding $T=7P=(2,0)$ jumps halfway around the route each time. The first step reaches $T$ and the second returns to $\mathcal{O}$, so $\operatorname{ord}(T)=2$.

The last example also connects to the doubling rule from Chapter 6. A point with $y=0$ is its own inverse, so

$$
2T=\mathcal{O}.
$$

The point orders $14$, $7$, and $2$ all divide the curve order $14$, just as Lagrange's theorem requires.

## The Public Base Point

A cryptographic parameter set designates one public generator of its selected subgroup, conventionally written

$$
G=(x_G,y_G).
$$

This selected generator is called the **base point**. It is not secret and is not chosen separately by each user. Its exact coordinates are part of the public curve parameters.

The terms describe two related ideas:

:::compare[Generator compared with base point]
- **Generator — mathematical property**: Its multiples reach every point in the group or subgroup being discussed.
- **Base point — public role**: It is the generator that a parameter set designates for everyone to use.
:::

For our toy parameter set, we choose

$$
G=(3,3)
$$

as the base point of the order-$7$ subgroup above. Its subgroup order is conventionally named

$$
n=\operatorname{ord}(G)=7.
$$

The base point generates the selected subgroup $\langle G\rangle$; it does not need to generate the entire curve group.

## Cofactor

Because the subgroup order $n$ divides the curve order, the **cofactor**

$$
h=\frac{\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)}{n}
$$

is an integer. Equivalently,

$$
\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)=hn.
$$

For the selected subgroup of the running curve,

$$
h=\frac{14}{7}=2.
$$

Think of $h$ as answering: **How many equal-sized copies of the selected subgroup fit inside the complete curve group?** Here, two seven-point cosets fill the fourteen-point group:

$$
\underbrace{14}_{\text{complete curve group}}
=
\underbrace{2}_{\text{cofactor}}
\times
\underbrace{7}_{\text{selected subgroup}}.
$$

The value $h=2$ therefore does not mean that only two curve points lie outside the subgroup. It counts two equal-sized cosets, one of which is $\langle G\rangle$ itself.

Production curves use an enormous prime $n$ and usually a small cofactor. The running values $n=7$ and $h=2$ reproduce that structure at a scale we can enumerate, but they provide no security.

## The Complete Toy Parameter Set

We can now collect the quantities developed across Chapters 5, 6, and 7. Reading down the table moves from the coordinate system, to the complete curve, to the selected route through that curve:

| Symbol | Meaning | Running value |
| --- | --- | ---: |
| $p$ | Prime field modulus | $11$ |
| $a,b$ | Curve coefficients | $1,1$ |
| $\#E(\mathbb{F}_{11})$ | Complete curve order | $14$ |
| $G$ | Public base point | $(3,3)$ |
| $n$ | Order of $G$ and $\langle G\rangle$ | $7$ |
| $h$ | Cofactor $\#E/n$ | $2$ |

The curve equation and the values

$$
(p,a,b,G,n,h)
=
(11,1,1,(3,3),7,2)
$$

form a complete toy analogue of the public domain parameters used by real elliptic-curve systems.

## From a Public Point to Protocols

The choice of $G$ gives every participant the same public starting point. In the simplest prime-order model, a private scalar is selected from

$$
1\le k\le n-1,
$$

and scalar multiplication produces a public point

$$
Q=kG.
$$

For the toy subgroup, only the six private scalars $1$ through $6$ are possible, so anyone can recover $k$ by trying every option. Production subgroups instead use an enormous prime order $n$. This gives the elliptic-curve discrete-logarithm problem a prohibitively large search space, while prime order avoids nontrivial smaller subgroups inside $\langle G\rangle$.

:::flow[From the finite-field point cloud to public curve parameters]
- Construct $E(\mathbb{F}_p)$
- Count the complete curve group $\#E$
- Follow the scalar cycle of a point
- Identify its generated subgroup
- Select a public base point $G$
- Record its order $n$ and cofactor $h$
:::
