[Elliptic Curves Over Finite Fields](/collections/computer-science/cryptography/elliptic-curves-and-finite-fields/elliptic-curves-over-finite-fields/) constructed the finite group $E(\mathbb{F}_{11})$ and used finite-field point addition to show that repeated additions of $(1,5)$ eventually return to $\mathcal{O}$. Cryptographic curve standards turn that behavior into public parameters.

## The Public Base Point

A standard selects one particular public point from the curve group:

$$
G = (x_G, y_G)
$$

This is the **base point**. It is not secret and it is not chosen separately by each user: its exact coordinates are part of the public curve parameters.

## Order and Generated Subgroups

Starting from $G$, repeated point addition gives the sequence

$$
\mathcal{O},
\quad
G,
\quad
2G,
\quad
3G,
\quad
\ldots
$$

Eventually, the sequence returns to $\mathcal{O}$. The smallest positive integer $n$ for which

$$
nG = \mathcal{O}
$$

is called the **order** of $G$. The points reached by repeated additions form the cyclic subgroup

$$
\langle G \rangle = \{\mathcal{O}, G, 2G, \ldots, (n-1)G\}
$$

If this subgroup is the whole curve group, then $G$ is a generator of the whole group. In real cryptographic parameter sets, it is enough—and common—for $G$ to generate a carefully chosen large prime-order subgroup.

For the running curve, the previous article showed

$$
G=(1,5), \qquad 14G=\mathcal{O}.
$$

The curve has $13$ affine points and one point at infinity, so $\#E(\mathbb{F}_{11})=14$. No smaller positive multiple of this $G$ is $\mathcal{O}$, which means $G$ has order $n=14$ and generates the whole toy curve group.

## Cofactor

For a base point of order $n$, the remaining ratio

$$
h = \frac{\#E(\mathbb{F}_p)}{n}
$$

is called the **cofactor**. For the running curve,

$$
h = \frac{14}{14} = 1.
$$

Many production curves similarly use a small cofactor, but the important security property is that the subgroup generated by $G$ has a large prime order.

## From a Public Point to Protocols

The choice of $G$ gives every user the same public starting point. In a protocol, a private scalar is chosen from $1$ through $n-1$, and scalar multiplication produces a public point such as $Q=kG$. A large prime $n$ keeps this subgroup from collapsing into a small, easily searchable cycle.
